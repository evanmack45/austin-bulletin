### The county's health tax comes in three sizes

<figure class="graphic">
<img src="/images/2026-09-03/central-health-rates.png" alt="Bar chart of the annual Central Health property tax bill on a median Travis County homestead valued at $372,638 under three rates the district is weighing for fiscal year 2027: about $470 at the no-new-revenue rate, about $496 at a 6 percent increase, and about $504 at the 8 percent increase the board set as its maximum">
<figcaption>Chart: The Austin Bulletin · Central Health budget figures, via KVUE</figcaption>
</figure>

Central Health, the Travis County healthcare district, has set an 8 percent increase over the no-new-revenue rate as
the ceiling for what it may charge next year. It has not chosen. On a median county homestead valued at $372,638, the
three rates still on the table come to roughly $470, $496 or $504 a year, and the top one raises about $49.3 million
more than the rate that would collect the same money from the same houses.

What the district says the money buys: $27.3 million in mental health spending, new inpatient behavioral beds, and a
$7.3 million reserve toward a crisis center it is discussing with the city, the county and Integral Care. "The place
where we deliver the most mental healthcare in our community is actually the jail," chief executive Dr. Pat Lee said,
"and our police officers are struggling to try to get people care that just doesn't exist in our community."

The ask lands while hospital money is short. Washington is holding back the supplemental Medicaid payments that
match what local hospital taxing districts like this one collect, and Texas hospitals have gone without since Tuesday.
Matt Mackowiak of Save Austin Now said the separate asks look small one at a time. "It's $1,500 more than it was three
years ago from all the entities for if you live in the city of Austin," he said.

<p class="whats-next"><strong>What's next:</strong> Central Health's board adopts its fiscal 2027 budget and rate Wednesday, Sept. 9; Travis County commissioners take up the proposal the following week.</p>

#### Money & bills

##### Abbott's office presses Washington over the hospital money Texas is not getting
Texas hospitals have been going without nearly $10 billion in supplemental Medicaid payments since the state fiscal
year began Tuesday, a delay the Texas Hospital Association puts at about $27 million a day. Gov. Greg Abbott's
spokesman Andrew Mahaleris said Wednesday that the state's financing "fully complies with federal law." The Centers for
Medicare and Medicaid Services said it is working with states on compliance. <span class="src"><a href="https://www.texastribune.org/2026/09/02/texas-hospitals-medicaid-greg-abbott/">The Texas Tribune</a></span>

<p class="good-thing">A group of University of Texas at Austin students spent two semesters trying to make a fabric coating out of corn protein and beeswax, and it works: water beads up on a treated cotton swatch and rolls off. The point is what it replaces. The chemicals that make clothes water- and stain-resistant are usually per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, or PFAS, which do not break down and which the students wanted out of the equation. Theirs does break down. "Essentially it creates a sort of waterproof finish," said Joanna Du, a junior chemistry major. The team came out of the university's Sustainable Fashion Materials research stream, where undergraduates get two semesters to chase an idea and see what happens, and they recently took the Outstanding Science Award at the Biodesign Challenge in New York, an international competition in biotechnology, design and sustainability — won this year, in that category, by a handful of Austin undergraduates and a jar of beeswax. <span class="src"><a href="https://www.kxan.com/news/science/ut-students-create-biodegradable-clothing-coating-from-corn-and-beeswax/">KXAN</a></span></p>
