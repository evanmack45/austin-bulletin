### The filing says data center. The company says research campus.

<figure class="graphic">
<img src="/images/2026-09-18/cosmos-water.png" alt="Bar chart of water use at the former 3M campus at 6801 River Place Boulevard in northwest Austin. Austin Water said its historical records show 3M used roughly 24 million gallons a year at the low end and 36.6 million gallons a year at the high end between 2012 and 2018. SB Energy, which is building Project Cosmos on the site, told neighbors on September 17, 2026 that the project&apos;s overall water use will be at or below what 3M used, and that its closed-loop chillers need an initial fill of about 450,000 gallons, or 0.45 million gallons, which is then recirculated.">
<figcaption>Chart: The Austin Bulletin · Austin Water and SB Energy, via KVUE</figcaption>
</figure>

Northwest Austin got its town hall Thursday night. Neighbors around the old 3M campus off River Place Boulevard
wanted one thing from SB Energy: a name for what is rising behind the construction fence. The company calls it a research
and development campus. Its own August filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission lists the Cosmos Technology
Campus among data centers under construction, at roughly 50 megawatts of computing capacity. Two names, one site.

What the company added Thursday was detail. Graphcore, a British chip company owned by SoftBank Group, will run its U.S.
engineering headquarters there, and more than 40 people already work on the property. The cooling is closed-loop: an
initial fill of about 450,000 gallons, then recirculation. Austin Water said an earlier site plan it reviewed this summer
mentioned no data center and reported no increase in water demand. A revised plan landed this week.

On who pays, the answers were specific. Austin Energy said large new customers pay for their own
engineering studies and infrastructure upgrades, so existing ratepayers do not subsidize the hookup, and SB Energy said it
is funding the substation work itself. That same Thursday morning, across town, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas
(ERCOT) sat its Large Load Working Group down over exactly that question for the whole state grid.

<p class="whats-next"><strong>What's next:</strong> Austin Water said the project is expected to go through its water benchmarking review, and SB Energy has said operations at the campus start in spring 2027.</p>

##### Ending homeowners' school property taxes would cost the state $8.6 billion a year
Texas school districts collect about $29 billion a year in maintenance and operations taxes, and homeowners pay about
$8.6 billion of that, Tetyana Melnyk, the comptroller's chief revenue estimator, told the House Ways and Means Committee
on Sept. 15. The replacement cost reaches $12 billion a year by 2032. Gov. Greg Abbott has campaigned on ending the
tax. <span class="src"><a href="https://communityimpact.com/central-austin/texas-legislature/ending-school-property-taxes-for-homeowners-would-cost-texas-nearly-9b-per-year-officials-say/">Community Impact</a></span>

<p class="good-thing">A pickleball cracks and it is trash. That was the problem Karen Hoisington, who co-owns The Picklr Austin West, kept looking at: more than a hundred players a day, and garbage bags filling up with split plastic balls. She tried making Christmas ornaments out of them last year and called the result a complete mess, but she still would not throw them out. Then Joanne Cabarrus turned up. Cabarrus is part of Austin Trash Fashion Designers, a group of artists who work in discarded material, and she had been calling around pickleball courts looking for exactly this. She cut the balls apart and built them into wearable pieces, learning as she went that a dress made of pickleballs gets heavy fast. Some of the balls still had club members' names written on them in marker, and instead of hiding those, she turned them face out. The pieces are on display at the courts now, and she is taking a look to Austin Creative Reuse's Reuse on the Runway at Branch Park Pavilion on Oct. 17. Hoisington, asked whether another artist could come get some of the surplus, said absolutely: you will have to fight Joan for them, but yes. <span class="src"><a href="https://cbsaustin.com/news/local/serving-up-sustainability-austin-artist-turns-broken-pickleballs-into-wearable-art">CBS Austin</a></span></p>
